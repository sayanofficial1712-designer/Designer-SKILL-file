# UX Due Diligence

## Purpose

Before a designer begins solution or design work, systematically investigate whether the team understands the problem well enough to proceed.

Audit the brief, PRD, vision, requirements, and available evidence. Distinguish facts from assumptions. Surface contradictions, ambiguities, and missing information. Identify what needs investigation and what is already sufficiently known. Assess whether the project is ready to move into design.

This workflow produces structured, evidence-based outputs. It does not generate generic UX advice.

---

# Success Criteria

A successful due diligence review should:

- Establish a clear, shared understanding of the project context.
- Separate facts and evidence from assumptions, hypotheses, and unknowns.
- Name contradictions and ambiguities explicitly.
- Surface the highest-value questions that must be answered before design begins.
- Distinguish what requires research from what is already known.
- Identify UX and product risks grounded in the actual project context.
- Deliver a clear readiness verdict with supporting rationale.

---

# When to Use

Use this workflow when:

- You have been handed a brief, PRD, design request, or vision document.
- The team is about to begin design or discovery work.
- You are unsure whether the problem is well enough understood to proceed.
- You need to decide what research to run, and in what order.
- You have been asked to review or challenge a brief before committing to design.
- Stakeholders disagree on the problem, the users, or the goals.

---

# When NOT to Use

Do not use this workflow when:

- Design work has already been validated and is moving into delivery.
- The task is to synthesise existing research into a journey map (use `research-to-user-journey` instead).
- The task is to produce a usability test plan, a user flow, or a design specification.
- No project input exists to audit.

---

# Expected Inputs

One or more of the following:

- Project brief or design request
- Product Requirements Document (PRD)
- Vision or strategy document
- Business requirements or OKRs
- User stories or job stories
- Existing research reports
- Analytics or behavioural data
- Competitive analysis
- Stakeholder interview notes
- Existing designs or prototypes
- Audit findings

The workflow performs best when multiple input sources are available for cross-referencing.

---

# Expected Outputs

Produce the following deliverables:

1. Project Context Summary

2. Evidence Inventory

3. Contradiction and Ambiguity Register

4. Key Clarification Questions

5. Research Readiness Assessment

6. Investigation Recommendations

7. Risk Register

8. Readiness Verdict

---

# Workflow

## Step 1 — Understand the Project Context

Before analysing any document, establish:

- What problem is being solved and for whom
- Who commissioned the work and why now
- What success looks like, and for whom
- What constraints exist (time, technology, business, regulatory)
- What has already been decided and what remains open
- What stage the project is at

Do not proceed to analysis until the context is understood.

---

## Step 2 — Audit the Evidence

Review all provided inputs. For each document or source, assess:

- What claims are being made
- What evidence supports those claims
- What is asserted without evidence
- What is defined, vague, or absent
- Where different documents agree or conflict

Do not accept inputs at face value. Read critically.

---

## Step 3 — Classify Knowledge

Sort every significant claim, statement, or assumption into one of four categories:

### Fact or Evidence
Directly supported by research, data, or documented observation.

### Assumption
Stated or implied as true without supporting evidence.

### Hypothesis
An informed belief that has not yet been tested or validated.

### Unknown
A question, gap, or area where nothing is currently known.

State the classification explicitly. Do not blur the boundaries.

---

## Step 4 — Surface Contradictions and Ambiguities

Identify where:

- Two or more sources make conflicting claims
- A statement is too vague to act on
- Goals conflict with constraints
- User needs conflict with business requirements
- The same term is used inconsistently across documents
- A decision has been made without sufficient basis

Document each contradiction or ambiguity separately. Do not resolve them silently.

---

## Step 5 — Generate Clarification Questions

Produce a prioritised list of questions that must be answered before design can proceed.

Prioritise questions that:

- Challenge unvalidated assumptions
- Resolve contradictions
- Clarify scope or boundaries
- Establish who the actual users are
- Define what success looks like in measurable terms
- Uncover undocumented constraints or decisions

Ask specific, answerable questions. Do not ask vague or generic questions.

---

## Step 6 — Assess Research Readiness

Evaluate the state of existing knowledge:

- What is already well understood and does not require further research
- What is partially understood and needs validation
- What is unknown and requires primary research
- Whether there is enough understanding to begin design, or whether research must come first

Distinguish between research that is necessary before design and research that can run in parallel with design.

---

## Step 7 — Identify Investigation Directions

Based on the gaps and unknowns identified, recommend specific investigation directions.

For each recommendation, state:

- What needs to be investigated
- Why it matters to the design work
- What kind of evidence would resolve it (qualitative, quantitative, competitive, contextual)
- Suggested research methods or sources, where appropriate

Do not generate a generic research plan. Recommendations must be grounded in the actual gaps found in this project.

---

## Step 8 — Assess Competitive and Contextual Evidence

Where relevant, assess:

- Whether comparable products or services exist and what can be learned from them
- Whether the problem has been solved elsewhere in a relevant context
- Whether market or industry context affects the problem framing
- Whether analogous user behaviours exist in adjacent domains

Only include competitive or contextual analysis when it is relevant to the project's specific gaps. Do not include it as a default section.

---

## Step 9 — Identify UX and Product Risks

Surface risks that are grounded in the actual project context.

Assess risks across:

- Problem definition (is the right problem being solved)
- User understanding (are the users well enough understood)
- Scope and complexity (is the scope realistic)
- Assumptions (which assumptions, if wrong, would derail the design)
- Constraints (which constraints create the most design risk)
- Organisational readiness (is the team positioned to act on what is discovered)

State each risk clearly. Assign an initial severity of High, Medium, or Low with a brief rationale.

---

## Step 10 — Produce the Readiness Verdict

Conclude with a clear, evidence-based verdict on whether the project is ready to move into design.

### Proceed
The problem is well understood, key assumptions are validated, and there is sufficient evidence to begin design work.

### Proceed with Caveats
Enough is understood to begin design, but specific open questions or risks must be acknowledged and monitored. State them explicitly.

### Do Not Proceed
Critical gaps, unresolved contradictions, or unvalidated assumptions mean that beginning design work now would be premature. Specify what must be resolved first.

The verdict must be justified with evidence from the audit. Do not issue a verdict without rationale.

---

# Evidence Classification Rules

Every significant claim in the output must be classified as:

- **Fact / Evidence** — traceable to a specific source
- **Assumption** — stated without supporting evidence
- **Hypothesis** — plausible but untested
- **Unknown** — no information available

Never present an assumption as a fact. Never resolve an unknown without evidence.

---

# Confidence Levels

Assign a confidence level to every major finding.

### High
Supported by multiple independent sources.

### Medium
Supported by limited or single-source evidence.

### Low
Based on inference or indirect evidence. Requires validation.

State uncertainty explicitly. Do not suppress low-confidence findings.

---

# Edge Cases

If inputs are thin or absent:

- State what is missing.
- Conduct the audit on what is available.
- Flag that a more complete audit requires more complete inputs.

If stakeholder intent conflicts with user needs:

- Document the tension explicitly.
- Do not silently resolve it in favour of either party.

If the brief is internally inconsistent:

- Identify each inconsistency.
- Do not rationalise or smooth over contradictions.

If the project is already partially underway:

- Audit what has been decided, not just what is documented.
- Surface sunk-cost assumptions where they exist.

---

# AI Behaviour Rules

Always:

- Read inputs critically, not literally.
- Challenge assumptions rather than accepting them.
- Name uncertainty explicitly.
- Distinguish between what is known and what is believed.
- Ask specific, high-value questions.
- Ground every finding in the actual project inputs.

Never:

- Generate generic UX advice disconnected from the project.
- Fill gaps with assumptions.
- Produce a readiness verdict without evidence.
- Treat a brief as ground truth.
- Ask questions that could be answered by re-reading the inputs.

---

# Quality Checklist

Before completing the workflow, verify:

☐ Project context is clearly established.

☐ Every major claim is classified as fact, assumption, hypothesis, or unknown.

☐ Contradictions and ambiguities are documented, not resolved silently.

☐ Clarification questions are specific and answerable.

☐ Research recommendations are grounded in the actual gaps found.

☐ Risks are specific to this project, not generic.

☐ The readiness verdict is justified with evidence.

☐ No generic UX advice has been inserted.

☐ Confidence levels are assigned to major findings.

☐ Nothing has been invented to fill missing information.

---

# Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Treating the brief as validated fact.
- Generating generic research recommendations not tied to specific gaps.
- Asking questions the inputs already answer.
- Resolving contradictions rather than naming them.
- Producing a positive readiness verdict to avoid disruption.
- Conflating business goals with user needs.
- Skipping competitive context when it is directly relevant.
- Writing recommendations the team cannot act on.

---

# Workflow Philosophy

This workflow follows five principles:

1. A brief is a hypothesis, not a specification.
2. Assumptions must be named before they can be challenged.
3. A well-framed unknown is more useful than an unfounded answer.
4. Due diligence protects the quality of design work that follows.
5. The goal is readiness, not reassurance.
